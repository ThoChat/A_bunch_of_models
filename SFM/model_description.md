# SFM — Social Force Model (with granular/panic extensions)

**Paper:** Helbing, D., Farkas, I., Vicsek, T. (2000). *Simulating dynamical features of escape panic*. Nature 407, pp. 487–490. DOI: [10.1038/35035023](https://doi.org/10.1038/35035023)

*Note: this is not the original 1995 Helbing & Molnár paper that first introduced the Social Force Model (Helbing, D. & Molnár, P., "Social force model for pedestrian dynamics," Physical Review E 51, 4282–4286, DOI: [10.1103/PhysRevE.51.4282](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevE.51.4282), cited here as ref. 16). It is the paper that made the Social Force Model famous: it takes the original 1995 model and extends it with "granular" body/friction forces so it can simulate physical contact in extremely dense, panicking crowds. It is one of the most-cited papers in the entire crowd-simulation field, and in practice it — not the 1995 original — is what most later papers mean when they cite "the Helbing social-force model."*

## How it works

Each pedestrian *i* is treated as a self-driven particle of mass `mᵢ` subject to Newtonian dynamics (`mᵢ dvᵢ/dt = sum of forces`). Three kinds of force act on every pedestrian at every instant:

1. **Driving force** — a pedestrian wants to walk at a desired speed `v⁰ᵢ` in a desired direction `e⁰ᵢ`, and accelerates toward that velocity with a characteristic relaxation time `τᵢ` (≈0.5 s): `mᵢ(v⁰ᵢe⁰ᵢ − vᵢ)/τᵢ`.
2. **Social (psychological) repulsion** — from the original 1995 model: each nearby pedestrian *j* and each wall *W* exerts a smooth, continuous repulsive force that decays exponentially with distance, `Aᵢ·exp[(rᵢⱼ−dᵢⱼ)/Bᵢ]`, representing the human tendency to keep a comfortable personal-space buffer — this force exists even when people are not touching.
3. **Granular/contact forces** (this paper's key addition, borrowed from granular-physics/hopper-flow models) — activated only when two people's bodies actually touch (`dᵢⱼ < rᵢⱼ`, i.e. the sum of their radii exceeds the distance between their centers):
   - a **body-compression force** `k(rᵢⱼ−dᵢⱼ)` pushing overlapping bodies apart, and
   - a **sliding-friction force** `κ(rᵢⱼ−dᵢⱼ)·Δvᵗⱼᵢ` that resists tangential (sideways) relative motion between two people in physical contact, exactly like friction between colliding granular particles.

These contact forces are what let the model represent real physical crowd crush — pressure building up, people being pushed and unable to control their own motion — rather than just polite spacing avoidance. An optional **herding term** (Eq. 4) lets each pedestrian blend between heading toward their own chosen direction and following the average direction of neighbors within a radius R, controlled by a single "panic parameter" `p` per person (p=0: pure individualist; p=1: pure herd-follower).

## What it's best for

- Simulating **high-density, high-stress crowd scenarios** — panic evacuations, stadium/venue exits, fires, stampedes — where actual physical body contact, pushing, and crushing pressure are central to the phenomena being studied, not just polite collision avoidance.
- Studying and predicting **specific dangerous emergent effects**: arching/clogging at exits, the "faster-is-slower" effect (people trying to move faster paradoxically slows the overall outflow), jamming at *widenings* of escape routes (not just narrowings), and the trade-off between individualistic and herd behavior when searching for an unknown exit.
- It is a foundational reference implementation still widely reused today (e.g. it is the contact-force model explicitly reused in the SPH_Crowds paper in this collection) — but it is comparatively simple/coarse and, per the authors' own admission, its parameters had to be hand-calibrated (no direct quantitative test was possible because suitable real panic data did not exist at the time — see Validation below).

## How it was validated

The validation in this paper is a mix of **parameter calibration against real (non-panic) pedestrian flow measurements**, and **qualitative comparison of the model's emergent phenomena to documented real-world panic/crowd-disaster observations** — not a trajectory-by-trajectory fit, since (as the authors state explicitly) no quantitative dataset of real escape panics existed to test against.

1. **Parameter calibration from real pedestrian data:** The paper explicitly states it "could not find suitable data on escape panics to test our model quantitatively," so instead the desired-speed and social-force parameters (`Aᵢ=2×10³ N`, `Bᵢ=0.08 m`, desired speeds of 0.6–1.5 m/s under relaxed/normal/nervous conditions) were calibrated to reproduce (a) the normal personal-space distance people keep at everyday walking speeds, and (b) the real measured bottleneck flow rate of **0.73 persons/second through an effectively 1-m-wide door** — both figures taken from **Weidmann, U. (1993), "Transporttechnik der Fußgänger,"** a widely-used pedestrian-traffic engineering reference (ETH Zürich). Pedestrian body diameters were set to a uniform 0.5–0.7 m range, approximating real human shoulder-width distributions (soccer-fan crowd). The contact-force constants `k` and `κ` were tuned qualitatively to produce reasonable pressure magnitudes.
2. **Qualitative comparison to documented real disasters/observations:** The model's simulated behaviors were compared against a set of nine characteristic features of real escape panics that the authors compiled from social-psychology literature, engineering handbooks (Predtetschenski & Milinski 1971), news media, and video footage of real events (the authors' companion website hosted such footage) — including observed dangerous crowd pressures of up to 4,450 N/m reported in real disaster analyses (Elliott & Smith 1993 on UK football-stadium disasters; Smith & Dickie 1993, *Engineering for Crowd Safety*), and observations of arching/clogging at real exits.
3. **Four concrete simulated scenarios**, each checked against these real-world qualitative observations:
   - **Room evacuation** (a 15×15 m room, 1 m-wide exit): reproduces irregular arch-like blocking and avalanche-like bursts of people leaving once desired speed exceeds ~1.5 m/s, and shows the "faster-is-slower effect" — leaving time for 200 people *increases* above a desired speed of ~5 m/s, and above that same threshold, people begin to be marked as "injured" (turned into stationary obstacles) once the summed radial force on them exceeds a pressure of **1,600 N/m**, a real injury-pressure threshold cited from Smith & Dickie (1993).
   - **Escape route with a widening** (a 3 m-wide, 15 m-long corridor with a central bulge): counter-intuitively shows that widening an escape route can *reduce* efficiency by up to ~20%, because the widening lets pedestrians spread out and then squeeze back together at the far end — reproducing a paradoxical real jamming effect.
   - **Smoky room with invisible exits** (90 pedestrians, two 1.5 m doors, no visibility): used to explore individualist-vs-herding behavior via the panic parameter `p`; results show neither pure strategy is optimal, matching real experimental findings on group problem-solving (cited from Kelley & Thibaut's *Handbook of Social Psychology*) that groups outperform individuals but masses are inefficient at finding novel solutions.
   - A companion set of dynamic simulation videos was published on the authors' website (http://angel.elte.hu/panic/) for visual inspection alongside these quantitative plots.

The authors are explicit that this validation is calibration/plausibility-based rather than a rigorous quantitative fit, and they close the paper by calling for future comparison against complementary real panic data as it becomes available.
