# SPH Crowds — Agent-Based Crowd Simulation up to Extreme Densities Using Fluid Dynamics

**Paper:** van Toll, W., Chatagnon, T., Braga, C., Solenthaler, B., Pettré, J. (2021). *SPH crowds: Agent-based crowd simulation up to extreme densities using fluid dynamics*. Computers & Graphics 98, pp. 306–321. DOI: [10.1016/j.cag.2021.06.005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cag.2021.06.005)

*(Extended journal version of the MIG 2020 conference paper "Extreme-density crowd simulation: combining agents with Smoothed Particle Hydrodynamics".)*

## How it works

This model doesn't replace collision-avoidance algorithms like RVO or the Social Force Model — it **augments** them so that agent-based (microscopic) simulation remains stable and controllable at extreme densities (≥4 people/m²), a regime where classical microscopic methods (contact forces between overlapping disks) become unstable or deadlock, and where crowds traditionally had to be simulated as a macroscopic fluid instead.

1. **Core idea — treat each agent as an SPH fluid particle.** Smoothed Particle Hydrodynamics (Gingold & Monaghan 1977; adapted for graphics by Müller et al. 2003) computes local density and pressure at each particle from a weighted sum over nearby particles (a smoothing kernel), then derives pressure and viscosity **forces** from these quantities. Every simulation frame, each agent additionally computes its local SPH density/pressure from its neighbors and adds the resulting SPH force to its usual navigation acceleration (from e.g. RVO or the Social Force Model) and to standard contact forces.
2. **Dynamic personal rest density.** Standard SPH assumes a fixed "rest density" the fluid wants to settle at. The paper instead gives each agent its *own*, time-varying rest density ρ⁰ᵢ (an exponential moving average of the density it has recently experienced, clamped to a range). This is the crowd density that the agent is currently "willing to accept" — a single intuitive parameter that directly controls how compressed the simulated crowd becomes, and it prevents the "splashing"/chaotic jittering artifacts that a fixed rest density produces at a crowd's boundary.
3. **Polygon-based obstacle handling.** Instead of the usual trick of sampling static "boundary particles" along walls (extra tuning, computational overhead), obstacle walls are handled analytically using the same 2D polygon representation already used for collision avoidance, approximating each wall's contribution to a neighboring agent's SPH density/pressure via a single representative point (inspired by, but simpler than, "volume maps").
4. **Density-dependent behavior blending.** Rather than always running SPH forces alongside collision avoidance (which can conflict — e.g. RVO tries to avoid all collisions, defeating the purpose of letting a crowd compress), the agent smoothly interpolates between a low-density "profile" (pure collision avoidance, e.g. RVO or social forces) and a high-density "profile" (pure SPH) based on its currently perceived density, so an agent behaves like a normal pedestrian in sparse regions and like a fluid particle once genuinely packed.

## What it's best for

- **Extreme-density and mixed-density crowds** (concerts, pilgrimages, evacuations with local densities reaching 4–10+ P/m²) where classical microscopic collision avoidance breaks down (deadlocks, unstable overlaps) but a fully macroscopic (grid-based, non-individual) fluid model would lose per-agent identity/goals.
- Scenarios needing an **intuitive density-control parameter** (the max rest density ρ⁰,ᵐᵃˣ) instead of tuning opaque contact-force stiffness constants.
- Reproducing **shockwave / crowd-crush propagation effects** (a pushing wave that travels through a densely packed crowd and displaces people involuntarily) in a microscopic (agent-preserving) simulation.
- It is explicitly *not* validated as being "more realistic" than existing methods in a general sense — the authors are careful to state they make no claim of superior realism, only that SPH gives easier, more intuitive density control and better stability than contact forces alone.

## How it was validated

The validation strategy is a mix of (a) grounding the scenario setups in real published crowd-dynamics measurements, and (b) internal consistency/ablation experiments comparing SPH-based profiles against classical ones — there is no direct trajectory-by-trajectory comparison to motion-capture data (the authors explicitly discuss this as an open challenge, since footage of extreme-density crowds is very hard to process into usable trajectories).

1. **Scenario 1 — Room evacuation** (400 agents exiting a 20×20 m room through a 0.8 m doorway). This setup is explicitly modeled on real published door-evacuation experiments: **Garcimartín, Parisi, Pastor, Martín-Gómez & Zuriguel (2016)**, *"Flow of pedestrians through narrow doors with different competitiveness,"* J. Stat. Mech., which reports real measured evacuation flow rates of 2–4 people/second and real observed densities around 4 P/m² (up to 10 P/m² in some cases). The paper compares seven simulation "profiles" (pure Social Force, pure RVO, pure SPH, SF+SPH, RVO+SPH, and the two density-blended variants) against these real flow-rate and density figures, reporting quantitative flow-rate/density tables (Table 1) for each.
2. **Scenario 2 — Crossing and bottleneck** (a mixed-density scenario: 224 agents evacuating a room while 8+4 crossing-flow agents must be avoided first). This is a synthetic stress-test (not tied to a specific published dataset) designed to demonstrate why density-dependent *blending* is necessary — showing quantitatively (Table 2) that pure RVO handles the crossing but stalls at the bottleneck, pure SPH handles the bottleneck but can't do the crossing, and only the blended RVO→SPH profile succeeds at both.
3. **Scenario 3 — Concert with shockwave** (10,000 agents converge on a stage, then a triggered push from one section of the crowd propagates a wave). The propagating-wave *behavior* (a wave that moves toward the stage, bounces off, and returns) is stated as being "consistent with real-world recordings," citing footage from a real 2005 concert and its published analysis: **Bottinelli & Silverberg (2018)**, *"Can high-density human collective motion be forecasted by spatiotemporal fluctuations?"* (arXiv:1809.07875), which analyzed video of that event (linked in the paper). This scenario is used to study how SPH's two parameters (gas constant k, max rest density ρ⁰,ᵐᵃˣ) independently control the wave's speed/thickness versus the crowd's density, and to show that SPH + weak contact forces together produce more stable results than either alone.
4. **Performance/scalability validation:** the concert scenario was benchmarked up to 30,000 agents on a 6-thread Intel Core i7-7920HQ, showing real-time frame times (≈18.6 ms per frame at 30,000 agents, under the 20 ms budget for 50 FPS), demonstrating tens-of-thousands-of-agents real-time feasibility.

In short: the *scenario setups* and *qualitative* wave/flow behavior are anchored to two published real-world sources (Garcimartín et al.'s door-flow experiments and Bottinelli & Silverberg's concert-shockwave video analysis), while the *quantitative* validation is mostly a comparative ablation across SPH/non-SPH/blended profiles rather than a direct fit to individual real trajectories.
